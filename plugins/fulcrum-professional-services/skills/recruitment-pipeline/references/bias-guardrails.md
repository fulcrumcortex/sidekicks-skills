# Bias guardrails for screening and shortlisting

Assess candidates only against the job criteria agreed at intake. Never use a protected attribute, or a stand-in for one, to source, screen, rank, describe or reject anyone.

## Protected attributes

Australian anti-discrimination laws (Commonwealth, state and territory, and the Fair Work Act) protect, among others:

- race, colour, descent, national or ethnic origin, national extraction, immigrant status
- sex, sexual orientation, gender identity, intersex status
- age
- physical or mental disability, including past, future or imputed disability
- marital or relationship status, pregnancy or potential pregnancy, breastfeeding
- family or carer's responsibilities
- religion, political opinion, social origin
- trade union membership or activity
- in some jurisdictions, irrelevant criminal record, and other attributes under state law

## Proxies to strip or ignore

| Proxy | What it can reveal | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Photo | Race, sex, age, disability | Do not request; remove from client-facing profiles |
| Date of birth, graduation year | Age | Do not request or show; assess experience against the criteria instead |
| Name | Sex, ethnicity | Do not use in assessment; keep it only for identification |
| Home suburb | Ethnicity, socioeconomic background | Assess only commute or location requirements the job actually has |
| Gaps in employment | Carer's responsibilities, illness, pregnancy | Do not penalise; ask about relevant skills, not reasons for gaps |
| "Culture fit" | Anything | Replace with specific behaviours listed in the criteria |
| Accent or "native English speaker" | National origin | Assess the communication standard the job requires, the same way for everyone |
| Visa type beyond work rights | National origin | Ask only whether the person has the right to work in Australia for the role's hours and duration |

## Client requests that cross the line

| Request | Response |
| --- | --- |
| "Someone young and energetic" | "I can assess energy-related requirements like the pace and hours of the role. I can't screen on age. What does the job need day to day?" |
| "No mums, the hours are long" | "I'll assess everyone against the hours and availability the role needs. I can't screen on family status." |
| "Must be a native English speaker" | "I'll assess written and spoken English at the level the role needs, for every candidate." |
| "Prefer a male for the site team" | "I can't screen on sex. What are the physical or licence requirements?" |
| "Only Australian citizens" | "I'll check the right to work. Citizenship is only a valid requirement if the role legally needs it, such as certain security clearances. Is that the case?" |

Record the request and the response in the job notes. If the client insists, stop work on that requirement and escalate to the agency's manager. Lawful exceptions exist (genuine occupational requirements, special measures, inherent requirements of the role); they are a decision for the agency with legal advice, never for the agent.

## Inherent requirements

A role can lawfully require what is essential to doing the job (a licence, the ability to lift a stated weight with or without adjustments, working at stated hours). Write these as criteria at intake. Ask every candidate the same question about them, and ask everyone whether they need any adjustments for the interview process.

## Assessment rules

- Every shortlist score cites evidence: a quote from the screening call, a CV line, a test result or a reference.
- Score "Unknown" when there is no evidence. Unknown is not "Not met".
- Use the same questions for every candidate for the same role.
- Do not rank on criteria that were not in the intake, even if the client mentions them later. Update the intake first, then redo every candidate's assessment against the new criteria.
- Review the shortlist for patterns before it goes out: if a whole group is missing, check whether a criterion or the sourcing channel is excluding them.

This is a practical checklist, not legal advice.
